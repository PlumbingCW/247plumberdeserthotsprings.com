Reverse Osmosis System Maintenance in Desert Hot Springs, CA

A reverse osmosis system needs three kinds of care: pre-filters changed on a schedule, a membrane replaced less often, and a yearly check for leaks and tank pressure. Skipping any of them lowers water quality or wastes water. Most under-sink systems are simple to maintain once you know the schedule.

What each part does

Parts of an under-sink RO system
PartJobTypical care
Sediment pre-filterCatches sand and particlesReplace on the maker schedule
Carbon pre-filterReduces chlorine taste and odorReplace on the maker schedule
RO membraneFilters dissolved material from waterReplace less often, when output or taste changes
Post carbon filterPolishes taste before the faucetReplace with the pre-filters
Storage tankHolds filtered waterCheck air pressure once a year
Drain line and saddle valveCarries rejected water to the drainCheck for leaks and clogs

Pre-filters protect the membrane. If they clog or wear out, chlorine and sediment reach the membrane and shorten its life. That is why pre-filters are changed more often than the membrane itself. Follow the maker schedule, and shorten it if your water is hard on filters.

A yearly maintenance routine

1

Shut off the supply and relieve pressure

Close the feed valve and the tank valve, then open the RO faucet until it stops.

2

Replace the filters

Unscrew each housing, install a new cartridge and tighten by hand.

3

Sanitize the system

Follow the maker instructions for a sanitizing solution, then rinse well.

4

Restart and flush

Open the valves, let the tank fill and discard the first tank before drinking.

Check the tank air pressure with a gauge when the tank is empty. Low pressure means a slow flow to the faucet. Most tanks hold a small air charge that can be topped up with a bicycle pump. Check each fitting for drips after restart, and look at the drain line for kinks.

Signs the membrane is wearing out

  • Water tastes salty or flat even after fresh filters
  • The tank fills very slowly
  • The system runs and drains constantly
  • A meter or tester shows the output has changed from when the system was new

A cheap handheld meter helps you track dissolved solids in the output. Note the reading when the system is new, then check now and then. If the number climbs, the membrane may need replacement.

Desert Hot Springs: read the water report first

Mission Springs Water District provides drinking water to Desert Hot Springs and publishes an annual water quality report on its website, including the 2025 report. Use it to understand what is in your supply, and test your tap before you decide on a system.

We do not quote a hardness or contaminant figure from memory. If your report shows something you want to treat, a system matched to that result will serve you better than a generic one. Our water softener and filtration page explains the options.

Look for systems tested to NSF and ANSI 58 for reverse osmosis claims, and note that drain and supply connections follow the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC).

Leaks, noise and low flow

The most common leak is at a push-fit tube connection. Pull the tube out, check that the end is cut square, and push it back in firmly. A leak at a filter housing often means the o-ring is dry or pinched, so lubricate it with silicone grease and reseat it.

A constant gurgle at the drain can be an air gap faucet or a clogged drain saddle. A system that runs without stopping often has a failed shutoff valve. If a leak stays after a few simple checks, shut off the feed valve and call a plumber. A leak under a sink can ruin a cabinet, as our faucet and fixture repair page explains for sink leaks in general.

A short care checklist for rentals and busy homes

Keep these on the cabinet door

  • Install dates written on each filter
  • The model number and maker phone number
  • The next filter change date
  • A spare set of pre-filters
  • The shutoff valve location marked with tape

We serve homes in Palm Springs and the wider service area. If the system is leaking or you cannot find the shutoff, call and we will help.

Choosing and testing your system

If you are buying a system, look for independent test marks on the box that show what the unit removes. A claim on a flyer is not a test result. Compare the claims to the items in your district water report, and pick a system that targets what you care about.

After you install or service a system, test the water. A simple meter that reads dissolved solids gives a quick check. For specific contaminants, a certified lab test is the right tool. Keep results with your system records, so you can see how the unit performs over time.
