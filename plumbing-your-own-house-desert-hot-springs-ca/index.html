Plumbing Your Own House in Desert Hot Springs, CA: What You Can and Cannot DIY

Many small plumbing jobs are fine for a careful homeowner, such as replacing a faucet, a toilet flapper or a showerhead. Jobs that change drains, vents, gas lines or the water heater are different. They often need a permit and an inspection, and mistakes can cause leaks, gas hazards or code problems when you sell the house.

A good rule is that if the job touches gas, vents, the main line or the permit rules, it is time to ask a professional.

Jobs most homeowners can handle

  • Replacing a showerhead, aerator or faucet cartridge
  • Swapping a toilet flapper, fill valve or seat
  • Replacing braided supply lines and under-sink shutoff valves
  • Clearing a simple sink or tub clog with a hand auger
  • Cleaning a trap or an aerator
  • Replacing a hose bib that has a shutoff behind it

Even these jobs need care. Shut off the water first and test the shutoff. Keep a bucket and towels at hand. Take a photo of the setup before you take it apart, so you can put it back. If a valve will not turn or a pipe is corroded, stop and call a plumber.

Gas work is the clearest line. A small leak is dangerous and invisible, and mistakes can be fatal. Leave gas lines, gas water heaters and gas appliance connections to a licensed professional.

Permits and inspections

A permit exists to put a second set of eyes on the work. An inspector checks that vents are right, that pipe is the correct size and that the work is safe. Unpermitted work can create problems when you sell, refinance or file an insurance claim. It is easier to do it right the first time.

Desert Hot Springs: what the Building Division says

The City of Desert Hot Springs Building Division lists repairing leaks in pipes, valves and fixtures, and clearing stoppages, as exempt from permits. It treats replacing or relocating a water supply, sewer or drainage system as work that is not exempt. It also says emergency work can be permitted after the fact by applying within the next working business day.

That is a helpful guide, not a substitute for asking. The division can be reached at (760) 329-6411. Ask about your exact job before you buy materials.

The rules for what is safe and legal come from the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and the local building department enforces them.

How to check a contractor license

The California Contractors State License Board offers a public license lookup on its website. Search by license number or business name, and check that the license is active and in the right classification. Ask the contractor for the number before you sign anything, and compare the name on the license with the name on the contract.

  • Ask for the license number and check it on the Board website
  • Get a written scope and price before work begins
  • Ask who pulls the permit and who schedules the inspection
  • Keep copies of the contract, the permit and the inspection sign-off
  • Do not pay the full amount before the work is done

Tools worth owning, and what to skip

A few tools handle most small jobs. A pair of adjustable wrenches, a basin wrench, a hand auger, plumber tape, a pipe cutter and a flashlight cover many repairs. Skip the chemical drain cleaners, which can damage pipe and make a later plumber visit unsafe. If a clog persists, our drain cleaning page explains what a professional does differently.

How to know when to stop and call

There are clear signs that a job is past a homeowner. A pipe that is too corroded to turn, a fitting that will not come apart, or water that will not stop after you shut the valves are all moments to stop. Forcing a stuck part usually makes the damage bigger.

Time is also a sign. If a job that should take an hour is in its third hour, the cause is probably not what you thought. A plumber who sees the whole system may spot the issue in minutes. Calling early costs less than calling after a flood.

  • Water that will not shut off
  • A smell of gas at any time
  • Sewage backing up in more than one fixture
  • A leak inside a wall or ceiling
  • Any job that needs a torch near wood or insulation
