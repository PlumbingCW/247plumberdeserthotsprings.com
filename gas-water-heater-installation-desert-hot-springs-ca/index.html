Gas Water Heater Installation in Desert Hot Springs, CA: A Homeowner Guide

A correct gas water heater install has six parts: the unit, a gas connection with a shutoff, a vent that rises to the outside, combustion air, a relief valve with a discharge line and seismic bracing. Skip one and the heater may work but fail inspection or become a safety risk. This guide explains each part in plain terms.

The six parts of a gas water heater install

What a correct install includes

  • A tank sized for your household and rated for your fuel
  • A gas line with a shutoff valve, a union and a sediment trap
  • A vent that rises, slopes upward and ends outside through an approved cap
  • Enough combustion air, especially in a closet or small room
  • A temperature and pressure relief valve with a discharge line to a safe spot
  • Straps that brace the tank against a quake

Each part has a reason. The sediment trap catches debris before it fouls the gas valve. The vent carries exhaust gases out of the house. The relief valve opens if pressure or temperature rises too high. The straps keep the tank upright if the ground moves, and a tank that tips can tear gas and water lines.

Venting is the part people get wrong

A gas heater burns fuel, and the exhaust has to leave the house. A vent that sags, leaks or runs too long can let carbon monoxide into the living space. Old vents also rust through at joints. If you are replacing an old tank, inspect the whole vent run and replace any section that is corroded or loose.

Install a carbon monoxide alarm near the heater and near the bedrooms. It is a small cost for a real safety margin, and many inspectors expect to see one. If your alarm is more than a few years old, replace it.

A rushed install

  • Reuses a rusted vent
  • Skips the sediment trap
  • Leaves the relief line short or capped
  • Misses the straps

A correct install

  • Replaces corroded vent sections
  • Adds a sediment trap and a union
  • Pipes the relief valve to a safe drain
  • Straps the tank to the wall studs

Size and recovery for a desert household

Tank size follows the number of people and how they use hot water. A small household may do well with a smaller tank, and a family that showers back to back needs more capacity. Gas heaters recover faster than electric ones, which helps in a busy home. A larger tank takes more space and costs more to heat when idle.

Garages are common here, and heat in a garage affects efficiency in summer. The unit also needs protection from vehicles. A raised stand and a guard post keep a gas pilot away from spilled fuel, and we discuss placement before we set the tank.

Desert Hot Springs: permits and the fault line

The City of Desert Hot Springs Building Division issues plumbing permits and can be reached at (760) 329-6411. Water heater installations are commonly inspected, so ask the division whether your job needs a permit before you start. We can walk you through what the inspector looks for.

The city also sits along the Mission Creek Fault, a branch of the San Andreas. That is why bracing matters here, and why we check the straps and the flexible gas connector on every install. A strapped tank is less likely to tip and break a line in a quake.

Venting, relief valves and gas connections follow the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and the heater maker instructions control anything the code leaves open.

What to ask a plumber before you hire one

  • Will you replace or inspect the vent, and who is responsible if it fails inspection?
  • Does the price include the relief line, the straps and the permit?
  • Will you haul away the old tank?
  • What does the warranty cover, and who files the claim?
  • Can I see your contractor license number on the California Contractors State License Board site?

What the installer should leave you with

When the job is done, you should know where the gas shutoff, the cold water shutoff and the relief valve are. You should also have the model and serial number, the date of install and the warranty paperwork. Take a photo of the data label and keep it with your home records.

Ask the installer to show you how to set the temperature. A setting around 120 degrees Fahrenheit is common for homes with children or older adults, since it lowers the risk of scalds. A higher setting gives more usable hot water but needs care.
