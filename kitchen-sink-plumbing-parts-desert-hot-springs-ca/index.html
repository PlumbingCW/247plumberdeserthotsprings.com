Kitchen Sink Plumbing Parts Explained for Desert Hot Springs Homeowners

Under a kitchen sink you will find supply lines, shutoff valves, a P-trap, a tailpiece, a basket strainer and often a garbage disposal and a dishwasher hose. A leak almost always comes from one of those joints, and the part that leaks tells you what to do. Here is each part and what goes wrong with it.

Find the leak before you fix it. Dry every joint, put a paper towel under each one, run the water and watch which towel gets wet first.

The supply side: shutoffs and lines

Two small valves, one hot and one cold, sit under the sink. They feed flexible supply lines that run up to the faucet. These valves should stop the water to the faucet when you turn them clockwise. Old valves seize or leak at the stem, and old supply lines crack or bulge.

  • Turn each shutoff once a year so it does not seize
  • Replace rubber supply lines that are cracked or older than ten years
  • Braided stainless lines are a durable upgrade
  • A wet stem or a drip at the nut means tighten gently and then replace the valve if it continues

If a shutoff will not close, you cannot work safely on the faucet. That is a good moment to call a plumber, since a stuck valve can break and flood the cabinet. Our faucet repair page covers valve replacement.

The drain side, from strainer to wall

Basket strainerSeals the sink drain openingDrip at the sink bottom from old putty

P-trapHolds water to block sewer gasLeak at the slip nuts or a cracked bend

Garbage disposalGrinds food and drains the sinkLeak from the flange or housing
Dishwasher hoseSends dishwasher water to the drainLeak at a loose clamp

The P-trap is the curved pipe that holds water. It blocks sewer gas, and it also catches rings and small objects. If a smell comes up through a sink, a dry or leaking trap is a common cause. Slip nuts need only hand tightening plus a quarter turn, since overtightening cracks plastic.

Which leaks you can fix yourself

Good DIY jobs

  • Tightening a slip nut
  • Replacing a worn washer in a slip joint
  • Swapping a supply line
  • Cleaning a trap

Call a plumber

  • A seized or leaking shutoff valve
  • A leak from the disposal housing
  • A cracked drain pipe in the wall
  • Repeated leaks after a repair

A leak that keeps coming back usually means the cause is not where it first appears. Water can run along a pipe and drip from a lower joint. We trace the source before replacing parts, which saves repeat visits.

This matters for what you pour down the sink. Grease and food solids load a septic tank quickly, so a septic home should be more careful. If a kitchen drain keeps clogging, our drain cleaning page explains what we check.

The trap and vent arrangement under a sink follows the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), so avoid swapping in an S-trap or removing a trap to speed up a repair.

Protecting the cabinet and the floor

A small leak can ruin a cabinet base in weeks. Particle board swells when it gets wet, and mold can start behind it. If the base feels soft or smells musty, tell the plumber, because a repair may need new flooring in the cabinet as well as new parts.

A cheap leak alarm under the sink gives early warning. Place it flat on the cabinet floor where a drip would land. It is a good idea in rentals and second homes, where nobody may notice a drip for weeks. We serve Palm Springs homes and others across the service area.

A simple monthly check that prevents big repairs

Once a month, open the cabinet and look. Touch each joint with a dry tissue, check the floor of the cabinet for dampness and smell for mustiness. Run the water and watch the drain for slow flow. A five minute check can catch a small drip before it becomes a rotted cabinet base.

Also check what lives under the sink. Cleaning products stored against pipes can corrode fittings, and boxes can hide a leak. Keep the space tidy and give yourself room to see the joints. A plastic bin under the trap catches drips and is easy to empty.

Choosing replacement parts that last

For supply lines, choose braided stainless steel. For the trap, a solid plastic or metal P-trap with a good washer set is fine. For the strainer, a stainless basket with a metal flange outlasts thin plastic. Buying a little better saves a visit later, and most of these parts cost little.
