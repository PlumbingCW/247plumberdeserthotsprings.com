The service line is the one pipe the house depends on. It carries water from the utility meter to the house, and it is usually buried and out of sight. When it leaks, the only clues are a wet patch, falling pressure or a bill that keeps rising.

Signs your service line has failed

Wet ground with no irrigation

A damp patch between the meter box and the house points to a buried leak.

Pressure that drops

Low pressure at every tap suggests a leak or restriction before the house.

A meter that runs with everything off

This is the classic test, and it points to the supply side.

Water noise near the meter

A hiss near the meter box or entry wall means water is escaping.

If water is surfacing or flooding the yard, close the shutoff valve at the house. If that valve is stuck or leaking, the utility controls the meter-side valve, so call our 24/7 line and we will help you decide whom to call.

Who owns which part of the line

In general, the utility owns the meter and the line up to it, and the property owner is responsible for the pipe from the meter to the house. Mission Springs Water District provides water in Desert Hot Springs, and its office is at 66575 Second Street and its phone is 760-329-6448. Call the district if the leak is on its side of the meter or if water must be shut off at the meter.

We repair the private side. If our locating shows the problem is on the utility side, we tell you so and you can call the district with the findings.

Before anyone digs

Digging near buried utilities needs a call to the regional notification center first. We do this as part of planning, but you should know it exists if a neighbor or landscaper suggests digging on a hunch. California requires notification before excavation, and utility locators mark gas, electric and water lines for free.

On sandy desert soil, a trench can collapse easily and soil washes out around a leak. That is another reason we keep trenches short and shored where needed. If the leak is inside the house, the burst pipe repair page covers it.

Service line depth, materials and shutoff valves follow the California Plumbing Code, which is based on the Uniform Plumbing Code (UPC), and the utility sets its own rules at the meter.

Pressure, regulators and meter-side valves

High pressure wears out pipes and fixtures. A pressure regulator at the house keeps water at a safe level, and it fails over time. If your faucets bang or your toilet fills noisily, ask us to check pressure. A gauge on a hose bib takes a minute and tells you a lot.

We also check the shutoff valve at the house. A valve that does not close fully is a risk, because in an emergency you need to be able to stop the water. We replace stuck or leaking valves as part of the repair.

After the repair

Once the line is fixed, we flush it, check each fixture for air and debris, and read the meter with everything off to confirm the leak is gone. We recommend watching the meter for a day or two. If your bill stays high, call us, since there may be a second leak.
